## Key Findings

### PRINCIPLE (principium)
- Latin root: primus "first" + capere "to take" → princeps "that which takes first" → principium "beginning, foundation"
- In Latin: original meaning "beginning, commencement, origin"
- In philosophy: Cicero uses principia for "foundations, principles, elements"
- Bentham (1789): "The word principle is derived from the Latin principium: which seems to be compounded of the two words primus, first, or chief, and cipium a termination which seems to be derived from capio, to take"
- Key usage: Cicero, principia rerum "first principles of things"; Aristotle's archai translated as principia
- Core metaphor: that which is taken first → foundational truth

### MAXIM (maxima)
- Latin root: maximus "greatest" (superlative of magnus "great") → maxima propositio "greatest premise"
- Boethius (c. 480-524) introduced maxima propositio in De topicis differentiis
- In medieval logic: maximal propositions are general principles underlying the correctness of topical arguments — the warrant for an inference
- Core metaphor: the greatest, most general proposition — a self-evident truth
- BYU: "maxim = from L. maximus 'greatest proposition' = Gk. axiom"
- Legal tradition: Latin legal maxims (pacta sunt servanda, summum ius summa iniuria) as foundational principles of Roman law

### TENET (tenet)
- Latin root: tenet "he/she/it holds" from tenere "to hold, grasp, keep, maintain"
- PIE root: *ten- "to stretch" — connecting notion between "stretch" and "hold" is "cause to maintain"
- Adopted from Medieval Latin where tenet introduced statements of doctrine
- Singular form (tenet) vs plural obsolete form (tenent, "they hold")
- Core metaphor: a thing held (to be true) — passive possession of belief, not active discovery
- Noah Webster 1828: "Any opinion, principle, dogma or doctrine which a person believes or maintains as true"
