**Leornian** — Old English *leornian* "to get knowledge, study, read," from Proto-Germanic `*liznojanan` "to follow or find the track," from PIE root `*lois-` "furrow, track." Cognate with German *Gleis* (track, railway line), Old English *læst* (sole of the foot), Latin *lira* (furrow).

The base metaphor: **learning is following a furrow or track.** To learn is to trace a path worn by others before you — to follow the furrow of what has already been found. The English *learn* descends directly through Middle English *lernen*.

Arthur Samuel borrowed the word for computing in 1959 — *machine learning* — capturing the sense that algorithms trace patterns (tracks) in data without being explicitly programmed for every case. The metaphor encodes both the direction (following what exists) and the effort (the furrow is cut by repeated passage).
