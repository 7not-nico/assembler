**Codex** — Latin *codex* (earlier *caudex*), from PIE `*kehu-d-` "cleaved, separate." Originally meant "tree trunk, block of wood" — a piece split from a log. Came to mean "writing tablet" (a split wooden board filled with wax), then "book" (bound wooden tablets), then "book of laws" (the Roman *codex* of legal statutes).

The semantic arc: something separated from a whole → a surface for inscription → a collection of written rules. English *code* descends through Old French, entering c. 1300 as "systematic compilation of laws." The computing sense (1946) inherits the idea of instructions systematically compiled into a form a machine can read.

The concept of *codex* is discovered, not invented. Wherever there is a need to fix rules in a durable, separable form — whether legal statutes or machine instructions — the *codex* form emerges. Machine code is a *codex* — instructions cleaved from the programmer's intent, inscribed in binary, read by a CPU.
