**Computare** — Latin *computare*, from *com-* "together" + *putare* "to reckon, prune, settle, clean." Literally "to reckon together, to sum up." PIE root `*pewH-` "to cleanse, purify" — *putare* carries the sense of "to make clear by pruning, to settle accounts by cutting away what is unclear."

In Roman usage, *computare* meant to calculate, count, or settle an account — bringing separate numbers together into a clear total. The word passed into French as *computer*, into English as *compute* (1630s). *Computer* (1640s) first named a **human** job: one who calculates. For 300 years, computers were people — women calculating ballistics tables at ENIAC, astronomers computing ephemerides by hand. The machine inherited the name of the human it replaced (c. 1945).

The semantic arc: to make clear by pruning → to settle accounts → to calculate → the machine that calculates. The *putare* root preserves the sense of *clearing away confusion* — computing is the act of making things clear through reckoning.
