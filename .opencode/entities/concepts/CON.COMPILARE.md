**Compilare** — Latin *compilare*, from *com-* "together" + *pilare* "to plunder, pillage." Literally "to plunder, to gather by pillaging." In Late Latin, the sense softened from "plunder" to "collect, assemble" — a *compilator* was one who gathered writings from various sources into a single work, often borrowing without attribution.

The word entered English through Anglo-French as *compiler* (early 14c.): "one who collects and assembles writings from various sources." Grace Hopper adopted it for the A-0 system in 1952 — the A-0 "compiled" subroutines from a library into a runnable program. The irony: *compilare* originally meant "to plunder"; a compiler takes from libraries without asking.

The concept of *compilare* is discovered wherever existing fragments must be gathered and assembled into a functioning whole. A compiler does not create from nothing — it plunders from what exists, arranges addresses, and produces machine code from the assembled parts.
