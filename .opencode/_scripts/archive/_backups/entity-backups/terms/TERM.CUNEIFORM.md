**Cuneiform** — one of the earliest known writing systems, originating in **Sumer** (southern Mesopotamia, modern Iraq) around 3400–3300 BCE. The script is characterized by wedge-shaped impressions (Latin *cuneus* — "wedge") made by a reed stylus on soft clay tablets, later dried or baked. Initially a logographic system for recording goods and services, it evolved into a mixed logographic-syllabic script capable of representing full speech, and was adopted for languages as diverse as **Sumerian** (a language isolate), **Akkadian** (Semitic), **Elamite**, **Hittite** (Indo-European), and **Old Persian**. The sign inventory shrank from roughly 2,000 signs in the early period to about 800 by the Late Bronze Age. Deciphered in the 19th century through the **Behistun Inscription** (by **Henry Rawlinson**, **Edward Hincks**, and **Julius Oppert**), cuneiform is encoded in Unicode as **Sumero-Akkadian Cuneiform** (U+12000–U+123FF), **Ugaritic** (U+10380–U+1039F), and **Old Persian** (U+103A0–U+103DF). It fell out of use by the 1st century CE, displaced by alphabetic Aramaic scripts.
