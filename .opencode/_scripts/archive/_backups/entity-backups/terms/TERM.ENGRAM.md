**Engram** — the physical substrate of a memory in the brain, consisting of an ensemble of neurons (engram cells) that undergo persistent chemical or physical changes during encoding and whose reactivation induces memory retrieval. The term was coined in 1904 by **Richard Semon**, who proposed that an experience activates a subset of cells whose enduring changes constitute the engram. Modern optogenetic and chemogenetic techniques — pioneered by labs such as **Tonegawa**, **Josselyn**, and **Rogan** — have demonstrated that stimulating engram cells drives recall in the absence of sensory cues, while ablating or silencing them erases the associated memory. Engrams typically involve distributed networks across the **hippocampus**, **amygdala**, and **medial prefrontal cortex**, and their study has become a cornerstone of contemporary memory research, integrating **synaptic plasticity** (long-term potentiation) with systems-level consolidation.
